From Photothermal Transients to Effective Transport Operators: An Operator-Based Interpretation of TTG Dynamics

Abstract 

Transient thermal grating (TTG) measurements probe thermal transport on submicrometer spatial and subnanosecond temporal scales, where departures from purely diffusive behavior have been reported in a variety of solid-state systems. Within the Mori–Zwanzig projection framework, such effects can be interpreted as manifestations of memory arising from microscopic degrees of freedom that remain unresolved at the observational scale. Motivated by this perspective, we formulate a forward model for TTG response in which heat transport is described by a linear evolution operator with memory. Microscopic transport processes are systematically coarse-grained into a non-Markovian transport operator, whose finite-memory form provides a general description of TTG dynamics, while diffusive and long-memory behaviors emerge as asymptotic limits of the same underlying framework. Within this formulation, the experimentally observed TTG transient is interpreted as the response of an effective transport operator rather than as a direct signature of a particular microscopic transport mechanism. The primary outcome of the inversion procedure is the determination of effective operator parameters, while microscopic transport mechanisms are inferred only through a subsequent, material-specific modeling step. The proposed Mori–Zwanzig inspired framework therefore establishes a systematic route from TTG measurements to effective transport operators and, subsequently, to coarse-grained descriptions of microscopic transport dynamics. 

Keywords: Transient thermal grating spectroscopy, Coarse-grained dynamics, Non-Markovian transport, Mori–Zwanzig framework, Thermal relaxation, Second sound, Hyperbolic heat transport 

I INTRODUCTION 

Photothermal and photoacoustic techniques belong to a broad family of non destructive methods that infer material properties from experimentally reconstructed signals generated following optical energy deposition [1-5]. Since the pioneering work of Rosencwaig and Gersho [1], the success of these methods has relied on the existence of a forward model connecting the measured response with the sequence of physical processes linking optical absorption, energy conversion, thermal transport, and signal formation [6-10]. Although the experimental observables differ among individual techniques, they share a common conceptual framework: material properties are inferred through the analysis of a transfer function relating an external optical excitation to the measured response. The central element of this transfer function is the propagation of optically generated thermal energy. 

Historically, this propagation has been described using Fourier’s law of heat conduction [11]. The resulting diffusive transport operator proved remarkably successful and became the foundation of modern photothermal characterization, enabling quantitative determination of thermal diffusivity, thermal conductivity, carrier transport parameters, optical properties, and a wide range of thermophysical quantities [12-16]. 

Over the past five decades, advances in laser technology, detection schemes, and signal processing have extended photothermal measurements toward progressively shorter temporal scales and smaller spatial scales. As a consequence, photothermal methods are no longer used solely as characterization tools. They have also become experimental probes of thermal transport itself [17-19]. 

At these scales, thermal relaxation processes [20-23], memory effects [17, 24,25], long-lived correlations, and other manifestations of non-equilibrium energy redistribution [18, 26-29] may become experimentally accessible. Understanding how such microscopic processes are encoded in experimentally observed signals has therefore emerged as an important problem at the interface of photothermal science, non-equilibrium statistical mechanics [30-32], and condensed-matter physics [33-36]. 

Among modern photothermal techniques, transient thermal grating (TTG) experiments occupy a special position [37-39]. The method combines high spatial resolution, high temporal resolution, and spatially periodic optical excitation. Unlike many photothermal configurations, TTG directly probes the temporal evolution of a single spatial Fourier mode of the temperature field. This simplicity makes TTG particularly attractive from the standpoint of transport analysis. At the same time, TTG experiments have provided some of the most compelling observations of non-diffusive thermal phenomena, including reports of second-sound propagation and related wave-like thermal responses [40-43]. 

Despite these advances, an important conceptual gap remains. The experiment directly measures the temporal evolution of a spatial Fourier mode, whereas the quantities of physical interest are the parameters of the transport operator governing that evolution. In most branches of photothermal science, material properties are inferred through a forward model that connects measured signals to effective transport parameters. For TTG systems, however, a general operator-based framework establishing this connection remains largely undeveloped. As a consequence, the relationship between experimentally reconstructed transients, effective transport parameters, and their microscopic interpretation has not yet been systematically formulated within a general operator-based framework [44, 45]. 

The present work focuses on the forward problem rather than microscopic mechanisms. Starting from energy conservation and a constitutive relation between heat flux and temperature gradient, we formulate a closed operator-based description connecting transport dynamics, periodic optical excitation, and the measured TTG response. Within this framework, microscopic transport processes are represented through a coarse-grained transport operator with memory, whose parameters provide an effective description of unresolved microscopic dynamics. The proposed formulation further shows that the morphology of TTG signals is governed not only by the structure of the effective operator but also by the spatial and temporal scales at which it is observed. Consequently, apparent signal morphology alone does not necessarily provide a complete characterization of the underlying transport operator. 

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II develops the operator-based formulation of TTG dynamics and derives the corresponding transfer function. Section III analyzes the resulting time-domain responses for representative memory kernels. Section IV examines the scaling behavior of the TTG signal and the influence of experimental observation scales. Finally, Section V discusses the results within the Mori–Zwanzig framework and Section VI summarizes the main conclusions.

Starting from energy conservation and a non-Markovian constitutive relation for the heat flux, we derive a closed evolution equation for the thermal mode. The corresponding transfer function is obtained in the Laplace domain, revealing a resolvent structure governed by the memory kernel.

IV. SCALING STRUCTURE OF TTG DYNAMICS 

The solutions derived in Section III describe the temporal evolution of individual TTG modes determined by the resolvent structure of the transport operator. However, experimentally observed signals depend not only on the operator parameters, but also on how these parameters are probed relative to the spatial and temporal scales of observation. In this section we analyze this scaling structure and its consequences for the interpretation of TTG measurements. 

IV.1 Asymptotic memory limits 

The short-memory and long-memory kernels define two limiting projections of the modal response. For the short-memory kernel (Eq. 15), the response reduces to a single exponential relaxation, where the temporal evolution is governed by the diffusive scale αq ². In contrast, the long-memory kernel (Eq. 31) produces an oscillatory response, whose periodicity is set by the spatial mode q and the effective propagation constant 𝑐 𝑒𝑓𝑓 .

These limiting cases provide reference responses for the finite-memory dynamics discussed below. Figure 1. Asymptotic modal responses. (a) Short-memory limit (Eq. 15), showing exponential relaxation of the selected TTG mode. Increasing 𝛼𝑞 2 accelerates the decay. (b) Long-memory limit (Eq. 31), showing oscillatory modal evolution. The oscillation period is controlled by the spatial mode q and propagation constant 𝑐 𝑒𝑓𝑓 . These responses define the asymptotic bounds of the finite-memory dynamics.

At the critical wavevector, the two poles of the transfer function coalesce and the response is described by Eq. (24). In this case, the resolvent exhibits a degenerate pole structure, and the resulting response remains monotonic, with no oscillatory contributions supported by the spectrum. In the asymptotic limits, variations of q merely rescale the temporal response. For the finite-memory operator, however, variations of q may change the qualitative nature of the dynamics itself, producing transitions between relaxational, critical, and oscillatory behavior. 

To separate intrinsic changes of the spectral regime from observational effects, we now fix γ and examine how variations of β and temporal sampling modify the experimental visibility of these responses.

The exact response can be decomposed into a fast transient contribution and a slow relaxation mode. Their crossover produces a characteristic concave-to-convex curvature transition in the signal. For smaller β, this transition occupies a larger fraction of the observation interval and is readily observable. As β increases, the transient contribution becomes restricted to earlier times and the curvature transition is progressively compressed. Finite temporal sampling further reduces the direct observability of this crossover. Sparse sampling may undersample the transient region and thereby obscure the curvature transition associated with the fast relaxation mode. However, the distinction between finite-memory and diffusive dynamics is not limited to this local feature. After the transient contribution has decayed, the exact response becomes increasingly well described by the slow relaxation mode. Although the resulting signal may appear nearly exponential, it remains distinguishable from the diffusive reference over a substantial portion of the observation interval through its different effective relaxation dynamics. 

Therefore, the disappearance of the curvature transition from the sampled signal does not imply the disappearance of memory signatures nor convergence toward diffusive behavior. Rather, it indicates that one particular signature of memory—the direct observation of the transient-to-asymptotic crossover—has become experimentally inaccessible. Information about finite relaxation remains encoded in the subsequent decay dynamics through the persistent difference between the finite-memory response and the diffusive reference. Only at sufficiently long times do the exact response, the slow relaxation mode, and the diffusive reference become practically indistinguishable. 

Supercritical modes ( 𝑞 > 𝑞 𝑐 .) 

In the supercritical regime, finite-memory dynamics are manifested through damped oscillatory behavior. However, the observability of these oscillations depends not only on the intrinsic system parameters but also on the temporal resolution of the measurement. 

As shown in Fig. 6, variations of β modify the oscillatory structure of the response while leaving the damping envelope unchanged. Increasing β reduces the number of oscillation cycles resolved within a given observation interval. Consequently, the response may appear progressively more monotonic, even though the underlying dynamics remain governed by complex poles and retain their oscillatory character. 

Finite temporal sampling further reduces the direct observability of oscillations. Sparse sampling may fail to capture successive extrema and thereby suppress visible oscillatory features in the measured signal. However, the absence of experimentally resolved oscillations does not imply the absence of memory signatures. Rather, it indicates that one particular manifestation of finite-memory dynamics has become inaccessible at the available temporal resolution. 

As illustrated in Fig. 6, both the sampled response and its damping envelope remain clearly separated from the diffusive reference even when individual oscillation extrema are no longer resolved. Thus, information about finite relaxation remains encoded not only in the oscillatory component itself but also in the envelope dynamics and the associated relaxation rate. The disappearance of visible oscillations therefore does not imply convergence toward diffusive behavior; it merely reflects a reduced ability to directly observe the oscillatory manifestation of the underlying memory process. Taken together, Figs. 5 and 6 demonstrate that finite temporal resolution does not eliminate memory effects but progressively limits access to particular dynamical signatures through which they are observed. In the subcritical regime, the transient-to-asymptotic crossover becomes increasingly difficult to resolve, whereas in the supercritical regime oscillatory extrema may become experimentally inaccessible. In both cases, the most prominent local signatures disappear first, while information about the underlying finite-memory dynamics remains encoded in the subsequent decay behavior. Consequently, the practical detectability of memory depends not only on the existence of finite relaxation but also on which signatures remain observable within the temporal resolution and observation interval of the experiment. 

V. DISCUSSION 

Within the Mori–Zwanzig projection framework, memory operators arise naturally when microscopic degrees of freedom that are unresolved at the chosen observational scale are eliminated from the reduced dynamics. This procedure yields a closed evolution equation for the experimentally accessible TTG mode, in which the unresolved microscopic dynamics are represented through a non-Markovian memory operator. In this representation, TTG measurements do not directly probe microscopic transport mechanisms. Rather, they constrain the resolvent structure of the effective operator governing the coarse-grained dynamics. The experimentally observed transient is therefore determined by operator-level properties, not by a one-to-one manifestation of a specific microscopic process. 

A key consequence of this formulation is that observed signal morphology does not provide a unique characterization of the underlying operator dynamics under realistic experimental constraints. Distinct combinations of memory and relaxation parameters may produce nearly indistinguishable transient responses when sampled over finite spatial and temporal windows. Conversely, identical operator parameters may yield different apparent signal shapes depending on the accessible range of wavevectors and observation times. 

Importantly, this ambiguity does not imply a loss of physical information. Rather, the information associated with memory effects and inertial (second-sound-like) contributions remains fully encoded in the transient response, but is redistributed across different observable channels. When temporal or spatial resolution is insufficient to resolve oscillatory or multi-exponential structure, these features are not eliminated but manifest instead as systematic deviations of the decay envelope from purely diffusive behavior. The unresolved dynamics are therefore embedded in the structure of the inverse problem rather than erased by projection or coarse graining. 

In principle, reconstruction of the effective transport operator requires the full transient response across multiple wavevectors and observation times rather than classification of individual signal shapes. The operator parameters are inferred from the global structure of the resolvent 𝐻(𝑞, 𝑠) ,whose response must remain consistent across the experimentally accessible range of scales. Apparent diffusive, relaxational, or oscillatory behavior therefore reflects the interplay between operator structure and observational scale rather than uniquely identifying an underlying microscopic mechanism. Robust inference of transport behavior consequently requires reconstruction of the effective transport operator rather than interpretation of signal shape alone. 

VI. CONCLUSION 

This work establishes a consistent forward model for TTG based on the Mori–Zwanzig formalism, explicitly linking energy conservation, non-Markovian constitutive relations, and the Laplace-domain resolvent of the effective transport operator. In this sense, the proposed formulation extends the conventional diffusive TTG framework to a broader class of finite-memory transport operators while preserving a direct connection between model parameters and experimentally measured transients. 

Within this framework, TTG experiments are naturally formulated as an inverse problem: the reconstruction of operator parameters from the full transient response across wavevector and time scales. This distinction is essential because the experimentally observed signal morphology reflects the projection of operator dynamics onto finite observational scales and therefore does not, by itself, uniquely define the underlying transport operator. This leads to a hierarchical inference structure. At the first level, TTG data constrain the effective transport operator through inverse reconstruction of its parameters from measured transients. At the second level, the inferred operator provides a coarse-grained representation that can be mapped onto classes of microscopic dynamics specific to the material under study. Crucially, TTG measurements constrain only the effective operator; any inference about microscopic mechanisms must proceed through this intermediate operator reconstruction step.
