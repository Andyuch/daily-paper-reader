(Dated: April 1, 2026) Quantum Hall edge excitations, whose low-energy behavior admits a chiral conformal-field-theory descrip-tion, are a promising platform for engineered dynamical experiments, including analog-spacetime proposals. However, establishing their edge dynamics in realistic electrostatic landscapes is essential for controlled dy-namical experiments and has remained experimentally challenging. Here we report spatiotemporal imaging of gate-controlled multipath dynamics of edge excitations in a 𝜈 = 1/3 fractional quantum Hall device using stroboscopic time-resolved photoluminescence microscopy and spectroscopy with ∼ 100 -ps resolution. By tuning a control-gate-defined potential landscape, we observe switching between mesa-defined and gate-defined trajectories and identify an intermediate regime in which a single launched excitation accesses multiple pathways. Time-resolved measurements at downstream locations reveal gate-dependent arrival times and pronounced tem-poral broadening, showing that the propagation dynamics are strongly modified by the local confinement and become increasingly dispersive in a multipath landscape. We further observe a long-range transverse optical response extending tens of micrometers into the bulk and persisting over distances exceeding 200 𝜇 m down-stream, consistent with the near-field component of an edge magnetoplasmon. These results establish direct experimental access to controllable multipath edge dynamics in the fractional quantum Hall regime and suggest a platform for engineered nonequilibrium and interference-based experiments, as well as future analog-spacetime studies in quantum Hall edge systems. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Recent advances in controllable condensed-matter and op-tical platforms have broadened the scope of analog experi-ments beyond their original astrophysical motivation [1–10]. Among candidate platforms, quantum Hall systems are partic-ularly attractive because their chiral edge excitations admit low-energy effective descriptions closely related to confor-mal field theories [11–16]. In particular, quantum Hall edges have attracted interest in analog-spacetime proposals, in which the edge serves as a one-dimensional analogue spacetime, the propagating edge magnetoplasmon plays the role of light, and designed potential landscapes define the effective geometry through which it propagates [17]. Real devices, however, depart substantially from idealized one-dimensional edge descriptions. Spatially varying electro-static landscapes can modify the trajectory, timing, and spatial profile of propagating edge excitations, thereby complicating both the interpretation of measured signals and the design of controlled dynamical experiments. Under static conditions, local probes such as scanning single-electron transistors and micro-photoluminescence (PL) have provided valuable insight into the bulk fractional quantum Hall state by revealing inho-mogeneity in the local electronic landscape [18–20]. Yet these approaches do not directly resolve how a launched edge ex-citation evolves in space and time as it traverses a realistic electrostatic environment. A related challenge is that the characteristic propagation speed of an edge excitation need not remain uniform across a structured device. As a qualitative local guide, the character-istic 𝐸 × 𝐵 drift speed associated with the confining potential gradient, 𝑣 D ≡ ( 1/𝐵 ) 𝑑𝑈 /𝑑𝑦 , sets a natural velocity scale and suggests that changes in the steepness of the confinement can correlate with changes in the observed propagation speed [21]. Although the velocity of an edge magnetoplasmon (EMP) is a collective quantity that also depends on electrostatics, screen-ing, and device geometry [22–25], we use the drift-based scal-ing only as qualitative guidance for trends with gate-defined confinement. In realistic devices, spatial variation of the po-tential landscape can therefore produce nonuniform veloci-ties, path-dependent delays, and enhanced temporal spread-ing, making direct spatiotemporal access to edge dynamics essential for engineered dynamical experiments. Here we report spatiotemporal imaging of gate-controlled multipath dynamics of edge excitations in a 𝜈 = 1/3 frac-tional quantum Hall device using stroboscopic time-resolved micro-photoluminescence microscopy and spectroscopy with 

∼100-ps resolution [28–30]. > II. RESULTS A. Device Geometry and Measurement Scheme

The device was fabricated from a wafer containing a 15-nm GaAs/Al 0.2Ga 0.8As quantum well (QW) [29], grown on a Si-doped substrate that serves as a back gate to tune the electron density 𝑛 in the QW. Unless otherwise noted, we focus on the 𝜈 = 1/3 fractional quantum Hall state, realized at 𝐵 = 14 T and a temperature of 40 –50 mK. The device geometry (Fig. 1) consists of a semi-circular mesa (radius 

50 𝜇 m) with straight extensions from both ends. The excitation gate, driven by an arbitrary waveform generator (AWG), is located upstream (right side) relative to the chirality set by 𝐵 .A semi-circular metal gate (the control gate) covers a semi-circular region of the mesa and comprises semi-transparent and opaque regions. Applying a DC voltage 𝑉 c to the control gate tunes 𝑛 beneath it; the semi-transparent region permits optical access for measurements of the 2D electrons directly below the gate. The AWG generates a bipolar voltage waveform whose am-plitude is varied across measurements. The waveform consists of a negative rectangular pulse with a width of approximately 

2 ns [28], followed by a positive component. Unless otherwise specified, the repetition period is ∼ 13 ns and the rise and fall times are ∼ 200 ps. Applying this waveform to the excitation gate launches an edge excitation that propagates leftward along the mesa edge, traverses the control-gate region, and continues along the downstream ungated mesa edge. Laser pulses (pulse width 1 ps; repetition period ∼ 13 ns), synchronized to the voltage waveform, are focused onto the sample to generate photoluminescence (PL) from charged ex-citons (trions) [31, 32]. Around 𝜈 = 1/3, the PL spectrum comprises singlet and triplet trion features depending on the electron spin configuration [20, 31, 32]. Although a quantita-tive understanding of PL spectra in the fractional quantum Hall regime remains challenging due to strong many-body effects, PL nevertheless provides a sensitive probe of the underlying 2D electrons and their environment [20, 28, 32]. Related optical studies further illustrate this sensitivity and its spectral man-ifestations [33–39]. The measured PL is accumulated over many repetitions rather than obtained from a single voltage pulse. Time-resolved dynamics are accessed stroboscopically by varying the delay between the laser and voltage pulses; we use three delay variables, 𝑡 1, 𝑡 2, and 𝑡 3, with different time origins due to experimental constraints (see Sec. III A and Refs. [28–30]).

𝑡 while varying the control-gate voltage 𝑉 c (Fig. 3). Although the signal at Point 1 is weaker than that at Point 2, the waveform is similar, consistent with reduced optical throughput under the semi-transparent gate. The reason why the edge excitation becomes undetectable in the time-resolved measurements of Fig. 3 while the spectral signatures persist is likely increased velocity dispersion during propagation. When the electrostatic potential in the control-gated region is tuned close to 𝐸 F, the condition 𝑈 ≈ 𝐸 F can be satisfied over an extended area under the gate. In an ideal disorder-free system this would correspond to a strongly de-localized edge location; in practice, however, the landscape is modulated by a weak disorder potential on the order of 

0.1 meV, commonly attributed to remote ionized donors in modulation-doped heterostructures [18–20, 26]. In this sit-uation, a single launched excitation can partition into mul-tiple trajectories defined by the disorder-shaped network of edge states. Because these trajectories sample different lo-cal confining-potential gradients, they acquire a distribution of propagation velocities, leading to temporal spreading of the excitation wavepacket. Given the stroboscopic repetition pe-riod of ∼ 13 ns, once the arrival-time distribution becomes comparable to or exceeds 13 ns the response from succes-sive excitations overlaps, reducing the visibility of a distinct dip associated with an individual pulse. This interpretation is consistent with the systematic broadening of the dip as 𝑉 c

approaches the range where the dip disappears (around −0.5 Vto −0.4 V) for both the mesa-defined and gate-defined propa-gation regimes. To interpret this long-range PL modula-tion away from the mesa boundary, we distinguish between the EMP charge-density oscillation near the edge and the accom-panying transverse electrostatic near field. In the following, “near-field” refers to this non-radiative transverse field com-ponent. The EMP is a collective excitation in which charge-density oscillations are inseparably accompanied by an electrostatic field. Nevertheless, the transverse near-field component has been far less explored experimentally than EMP propagation along the edge. This is largely because the near field is evanes-cent (non-radiative) and conventional electrical probes pre-dominantly access signals integrated along the propagation direction, with limited sensitivity to the spatial distribution of the field extending into the bulk [22, 24, 25, 40, 41]. In contrast, the trion PL response provides a local optical readout of the 2DEG environment, enabling direct visualization of the long-range transverse influence associated with the EMP near field. In summary, we have directly visualized delocalized edge excitations propagating along multiple paths in the fractional quantum Hall regime. By tuning the gate-induced potential landscape, we controlled the edge trajectory and observed propagation velocity dispersion consistent with multi-path propagation shaped by disorder-induced potential variations. Our observations also revealed a long-range near-field mode extending deep into the bulk, with a spatial profile and weak attenuation over our measured range that are consistent with the characteristics of the EMP. These results identify a regime of edge dynamics in fractional quantum Hall systems char-acterized by long-range multi-path propagation. This regime suggests a platform for analog quantum simulations of emer-gent spacetime geometries and may enable interference-based experiments in quantum Hall edge systems once phase coher-ence is established and verified. More broadly, the ability to define multiple excitation pathways dynamically motivates analogies with proposals in which spacetime itself does not follow a single, fixed geometry but is instead described by a quantum superposition of multiple geometries, with different edge-excitation pathways playing the role of alternative effec-tive geometrical backgrounds.   

> III. METHODS A. Experimental methods

Laser pulses (pulse width ∼ 1 ps; repetition period 𝑇 rep ≈

13 ns) were generated by a mode-locked Ti:sapphire laser and synchronized to the voltage waveform applied from the AWG. The laser beam was focused onto the sample through an objective lens, yielding a diffraction-limited spot size of 0.77 𝜇 m. The emitted PL was collected by the same objective lens and sent to a spectrometer equipped with a CCD detector. The focal position was controlled using 0009 009piezoelectric stages. Each spectrum was accumulated over 𝑁 rep = 𝑇 exp /𝑇 rep 

repetitions, with a total exposure time of 𝑇 exp = 10 –20 s. The relative delay between the laser and voltage pulses was controlled electronically to enable stroboscopic time-resolved measurements; the definitions of 𝑡 1, 𝑡 2, and 𝑡 3 are provided in the Supplemental Material.  

> B. Effects of Metal Gate Deposition and Sidewall Depletion

Depositing a metal gate on the GaAs surface is known to modify the local electrostatic potential through the Schottky barrier determined by the metal work function and the pinning of surface states [42, 43], resulting in an offset relative to the bare GaAs surface. In addition, the etched mesa sidewall can induce lateral depletion and a corresponding local potential increase, particularly at low carrier density [44–46]. This sidewall-induced potential modulation can extend over several micrometers from the mesa edge [47], facilitating depletion in the adjacent region. The combination of the metal-induced surface-potential offset and sidewall depletion can therefore produce a non-negligible built-in electrostatic modulation even at 𝑉 c = 0,which can shift the effective edge position toward the gate boundary and allow edge excitations to propagate along the gate-defined path. Consistent with this interpretation, a posi-tive control-gate voltage of approximately +0.3 V is required to 7compensate this built-in offset and confine the edge excitation strictly to the mesa-defined path. 

C. Estimation of Electrochemical Potential Shift 

The change in the 2DEG density induced by the control-gate voltage can be estimated using a parallel-plate capacitor model, 𝑒 Δ𝑛 𝑒 = 𝐶 Δ𝑉 , where 𝐶 is the capacitance per unit area between the control gate and the 2DEG. Using the device ge-ometry, we obtain Δ𝑛 𝑒 /Δ𝑉 = 3.9 × 10 11 cm −2/V. To convert this density change into an energy scale, we use the zero-field 2D relation between carrier density and chemical potential, 

Δ𝜇 ≃ ( 𝜋 ℏ2/𝑚 ∗)Δ𝑛 𝑒 , where 𝑚 ∗ = 0.067 𝑚 𝑒 is the GaAs effec-tive mass. This yields an estimated electrochemical-potential shift of Δ𝜇 ∼ 4 meV for Δ𝑉 ∼ 0.3 V (the range over which two paths coexist in Figs. 2(d) and 2(e)) and Δ𝜇 ∼ 3 meV 

for the excitation-gate pulse amplitude Δ𝑉 ∼ ± 0.1 V. These estimates are intended as order-of-magnitude energy scales; in the quantum Hall regime the relation between Δ𝑛 𝑒 and Δ𝜇 

can be modified by Landau quantization and screening, but the comparison of the relevant voltage scales remains valid.
